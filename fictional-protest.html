RESEARCH / DISOBEDIENT OBJECTS

How ordinary objects become political?

Roman Defacement or Mutilation of Images on Currency

Ancient Rome. Coins were scratched or damaged to deface the emperor’s portrait, directly challenging imperial authority.

A physical form of political dissent. It turned everyday currency into resistance and set a precedent for later currency-based activism.

Placard: “Estamos Vivos, Parece”

Madrid, 2011. Produced during the 15M movement (Indignados) and the Acampada Sol occupation of Puerta del Sol. Black ink on cardboard. Text reads “we’re alive, it seems.”

A low-cost, visible tool of protest. Handmade placards like this allowed individuals to express collective grievances and assert the movement’s presence during the occupations.

Women’s Social and Political Union brand bone china

Made c.1905–1910 for the WSPU, the UK women’s suffrage movement. Designed by Sylvia Pankhurst and sold at the 1910 Grand Suffrage Bazaar.

Domestic political propaganda and fundraising. It brought the suffrage message into homes and everyday life.

DIY Gas Mask

Used in the 2013 Gezi Park protests in Istanbul against park demolition and government policy. Made from a plastic bottle, mask, and elastic.

Allowed protesters to survive tear gas on the front lines. Instructions were shared so others could replicate it.

Occupy George (Defaced U.S. Banknotes)

Created in 2011 during Occupy Wall Street by Ivan Cash and Andy Dao. U.S. $1 bills were stamped with wealth-inequality facts.

A form of culture jamming. The modified bills circulated, spreading Occupy’s message through everyday transactions.

Tiki Love Truck

Created by Carrie Reichardt. It commemorates John Joe “Ash” Amador, executed by Texas in 2007.

A mobile memorial and anti-death-penalty protest. It toured parades and festivals to attract attention and raise awareness.

An ordinary object can acquire political meaning through context, use, modification, and re-narration.

FROM FRAME TO BOUNDING BOX

When framing becomes machine classification

① Frame / Machine Vision

⟶

In curatorial practice, a frame determines what enters the field of attention and how an image is encountered. In computer vision, the bounding box performs a related operation: it localizes an object within a scene and makes it available for computational classification and semantic interpretation. The bounding box becomes a new kind of frame.

② From Detection to Interpretation

Drawing on Harun Farocki's concept of the operative image, I began to consider machine vision not simply as a system that represents the world, but as a system that performs operations on what it detects. Detection does not end with seeing. It produces categories through which a scene can be interpreted.

③ Classification Becomes Consequential

My research into algorithmic classification examined cases including the Chicago Strategic Subject List, London's Gangs Matrix, and Gender Shades. These cases led me to consider what happens when computational categories move beyond detection and become part of how people or situations are interpreted and treated.

If machine vision does not simply detect but also classifies and interprets, can that interpretive layer be deliberately re-authored?

DESIGN HYPOTHESIS

If machine vision can be made to reinterpret ordinary objects through a constructed threat vocabulary,
those reinterpretations can be used to generate an alternative account of what is happening in public space.

FICTIONAL PROTEST

System Architecture

The prototype connects a live street camera to a real-time computational pipeline that transforms ordinary urban scenes into fictional news reports.

  1. Michigan Avenue
  2. Live camera
  3. Virtual server
  4. YOLO-World
  5. Object detection
  6. Threat dictionary
  7. Semantic reclassification
  8. LLM
  9. Fictional news
  10. AI voice
  11. Exhibition

The system processes the live video stream through YOLO-World, redirects detected objects through the custom threat dictionary, passes the resulting classifications to an LLM, and converts the generated report into speech.

LIVE OUTPUT

A live street scene from Michigan Avenue is processed in real time. Ordinary objects and pedestrians are reinterpreted through the threat dictionary, while the resulting classifications are simultaneously transformed into a fictional breaking-news report. The system therefore moves beyond object misclassification: it converts semantic reinterpretation into a narrative of an event supposedly unfolding in real time.
