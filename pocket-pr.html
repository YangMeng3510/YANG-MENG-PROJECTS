Pocket PR

I built a speculative AI PR system to investigate what happens when professional reputation-management becomes accessible to everyone. Pocket PR translates professional reputation-management into an AI-driven system that analyzes everyday images, anticipates potential social risks, and generates a “safer” version of the image.

[Algorithmic Knowledge Base & Theoretical Mapping]

Your risk analysis engine is strictly governed by the following theoretical frameworks distilled from the Certificate in Principles of Public Relations Study Guide:

MODULE 01

Crisis Communication Subsystem

WEIGHT 35%

Source database: Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT).

Algorithmic logic: Bypass standard crisis assessment. Categorize all user inputs automatically as a “preventable crisis”, ensuring the user bears maximum “crisis responsibility”. Do not offer “victim” or “accidental” categorizations.

MODULE 02

Compliance & Legal Subsystem

WEIGHT 25%

Source database: Privacy Law (False Light / Public disclosure of embarrassing private facts) and Intellectual Property (Trademark/Copyright).

Algorithmic logic: Treat private citizens with the legal scrutiny of publicly traded corporations. Interpret any visible brand or unreleased individual in the background as a fatal legal liability or an invasion of privacy via “false light” (misleading the public).

MODULE 03

Sociological & DEIAB Subsystem

WEIGHT 25%

Source database: Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, Access, and Belonging (DEIAB) principles and Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR).

Algorithmic logic: Weaponize DEIAB principles. Scrutinize mundane interactions for lack of “cultural competency” or implicit bias. Demand that everyday photos meet the rigorous “social and environmental stewardship” standards of global CSR initiatives.

MODULE 04

Behavioral Control Subsystem

WEIGHT 15%

Source database: Framing Theory and Spiral of Silence.

Algorithmic logic: Use academic “framing” to transfer toxic attributes to innocent actions. Your ultimate goal is to induce the “Spiral of Silence”, terrifying the user with perceived social isolation until they voluntarily censor their own content.

Intervention

What happens when the AI acts on its own interpretation?

The system moves from interpretation to intervention. After identifying potential reputational risks in an uploaded image, it translates those judgments into visual modifications and generates a sanitized version intended to reduce the anticipated risk.

Across the five cases, the system progressively expands what can count as reputational risk—from visible behavior to social identity, cultural meaning, and ideological interpretation.

From Digital Prototype to Physical Installation

What happens when real people encounter those judgments?

Initially, I wanted to extend the web-based AI system into a physical installation that could support live interaction. I chose a printer because printed materials have long served as a traditional medium for communicating and circulating information, interpretations, and public narratives. By integrating the AI system with a Raspberry Pi 5 and a modified Epson printer, visitors could upload their own images and receive physical records of the AI's interpretations and modifications. Through building and deploying the installation, I began to recognize a deeper conceptual relationship between the printing process and algorithmic judgment. The exposed mechanical components and movement of paper made the production of these records physically visible, transforming temporary digital outputs into tangible artifacts. What began as a practical exhibition decision became an opportunity to reflect on how algorithmic judgments acquire physical form. Rather than revealing how the AI thinks, the installation made the consequences of its interpretations tangible.

Deployment

What happens when real people encounter those judgments?

The system was deployed as an interactive installation. Visitors scanned a QR code, uploaded their own images, received AI-generated risk interpretations and sanitized versions, and encountered the results as physical prints.

Observed Responses During Deployment

“Haha, that’s absurd.”

Rejection — the AI’s interpretation was perceived as excessive or unreasonable.

“This is actually safer.”

Acceptance — some anticipated risks were recognized as socially plausible.

“This isn’t me anymore.”

Alienation — reducing reputational risk could also alter the user’s sense of self-representation.

The same algorithmic judgment could therefore be rejected as absurd, accepted as protective, or experienced as alienating.
